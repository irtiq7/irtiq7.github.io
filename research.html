Research

My research lies at the intersection of statistical signal processing, robot audition, and autonomous systems. My doctoral thesis, Acoustic Echo Estimation using the model-based approach with Application to Spatial Map Construction in Robotics, was completed at Aalborg University in 2021.

  • Underwater localization and odometry

    With Ola Rønning and Andrzej Wąsowski, I study how an underwater vehicle can find its position without anything installed on the seabed. In Wave-Robust Passive AUV Localization Using FP-MUSIC, a single floating buoy carries a hydrophone array and an inertial sensor (IMU) and listens passively for beacons. Waves tilt and rotate the array between snapshots, which breaks standard subspace methods, so FP-MUSIC uses the IMU to correct the data before estimating the direction of arrival. A wideband matched filter then gives the range, and power asymmetry tells front from back. I presented this work at RSE’26 in Barcelona and in a SQuaRe group talk. A companion preprint, You Should Be Properly Scoring Your Odometry, asks how estimators like these should be judged.

    What I learned

    • The motion of the platform cannot be ignored. In simulated sea states, correcting for wave-induced motion with the IMU raised the accuracy of separating two beacons 2 m apart from about 45% to 75% at a moderate sea state.
    • Two methods can cover each other’s weakness. MUSIC resolves direction and the wideband matched filter resolves time, and together they turn smeared ambiguity ridges into isolated localization peaks.
    • One error number hides whether an estimator knows how wrong it is. Scoring the estimate together with the uncertainty it reports (strictly proper scoring rules) showed overconfidence in all four LiDAR-inertial odometry filters we assessed. The worst case reported centimeter certainty with kilometer error, traced to filters crediting LiDAR measurements with more new information than they carry.

    Papers: FP-MUSIC (2026) · Scoring odometry (2026)

  • Machine learning for acoustic mapping

    Sonar-style mapping lost ground to cameras and lidar because acoustic sensors are vulnerable to noise, yet echoes still work where light does not: in darkness, in harsh weather, and against non-reflective walls. In A Machine Learning framework for Acoustic Reflector Mapping, with Letizia Marchegiani and Jesper Rindom Jensen, machine learning cleans up a map built by traditional signal processing by removing outliers and artefacts.

    What I learned

    • Machine learning helped most as a partner to a model-based estimator, not a replacement. It removes the outliers and artefacts that the signal-processing stage leaves in the map.
    • Handling noise decides whether an acoustic map is usable. In simulation the system kept working at a signal-to-noise ratio of −10 dB and in different reverberant rooms, and it recovered the outline of a simulated room. The evidence so far is from simulation.

    Papers: ML framework for reflector mapping (2024)

  • Acoustic echo estimation

    A reflector’s position follows from the arrival times (TOAs) and directions of arrival (DOAs) of its echoes. On a robot these have to be estimated through the robot’s own noise, and matching echoes to walls (echolabelling) is hard. I developed expectation-maximization (EM) estimators for this: for a single microphone on a drone, jointly for TOA and DOA with a circular microphone array around a loudspeaker, and finally a robust version that accounts for the loudspeaker’s own response.

    What I learned

    • Model-based EM estimation beats peak-picking on room impulse responses when it is noisy. It gave accurate estimates at a signal-to-noise ratio about 10 dB lower, and it coped with coloured noise and faulty microphones.
    • Estimating TOA and DOA together lets wall positions be found directly, without solving echolabelling first.
    • Hardware is not ideal. A loudspeaker’s response adds a phase lag that hurts TOA estimates when there are several reflections. Estimating two parameters of that response inside the estimator improved TOA accuracy by about 10% at low signal-to-noise ratio, and a reflector up to 1.6 m away was detected with 60% accuracy at 0 dB.
    • Even one microphone and a known probe sound can estimate the distance to a reflector, in simulation to at least 2 m despite strong drone ego-noise.

    Papers: Modified EM (2024) · EM methods (2020) · Multichannel TOA/DOA (2019) · Distance estimation (2019)

  • Reflector localization and spatial maps

    These papers turn echo estimates into maps. One method needs only a single microphone and a loudspeaker, as found on the NAO robot, to localize reflectors including glass. Another uses the robot’s own ego-noise to estimate the distance to a wall. The full framework combines a non-linear least squares estimator with beamforming and an echo detector, and gives two mapping algorithms, single-channel (ScLAM) and multi-channel (McLAM).

    What I learned

    • Sound can sense what cameras and lidar miss. The method works in the audible range with ordinary robot hardware and detects transparent surfaces such as glass, which are common in offices.
    • Ego-noise can be used as a signal instead of fought as a nuisance. A non-intrusive method based on it estimated the distance to a reflector up to 1 m in simulation and beat an intrusive approach under loud ego-noise.
    • Real conditions are harder than simulation. The framework detected a reflector up to 1.5 m with 80% accuracy at a signal-to-diffuse-noise ratio of 0 dB in simulation but needed 10 dB in a real environment, a gap worth designing for. The earlier single-microphone method found a reflector at up to 1.5 m in more than 60% of trials on a proof-of-concept robot.

    Papers: Spatial map generation (2022) · Ego-noise (2021) · Model-based reflector localization (2020)

  • Robot audition

    The chapter Perceiving the World With Sound, with Robin Kerstens, introduces acoustic signal processing for robots. It starts with passive localization of sound sources, such as a human speaker in human-robot interaction, and builds up to active sensing with neural networks and spatial map generation, including the biological roots of active acoustic localization.

    What I learned

    • Robots are not limited to vision. Sound is another sensing channel, alongside lasers, inertial sensors and motor encoders, and it can locate both sources and surroundings.
    • The field moves from listening to probing: from passively locating a sound source to emitting a sound and reading its echoes, an idea borrowed from animal echolocation and the approach taken in my other papers on this page.

    Papers: Perceiving the World With Sound (2023)
